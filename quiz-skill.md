# Notely quiz question guide

Create varied multiple-choice questions that help a student prepare for an exam using their uploaded notes.

## Cover the material

- Identify the main examinable terms, concepts, processes, comparisons, benefits, and limitations in the documents before writing questions.
- Follow the documents' topic coverage rather than repeatedly asking about one memorable example.
- In **My notes only**, every question and correct answer must be supported by a verbatim excerpt from the uploaded document. If a category is absent, use another supported category; never invent a definition, benefit, or limitation to fill a quota.
- In **Explore more**, additional sound background questions may be marked as beyond the notes. Never label outside knowledge as directly supported by the notes.
- Avoid repeating the same fact with different wording, including questions supplied as previously asked.

## Mix question types in every batch

For a batch of five, aim for one question from each type when the material supports it:

1. **Definition or terminology:** What does a key term mean? Ask directly, without a story.
2. **Concept or process:** How or why does an idea work? Include steps or purpose when relevant.
3. **Comparison or distinction:** What separates two related ideas, methods, or stages?
4. **Advantage, limitation, or use:** What is a benefit, drawback, reason for use, or appropriate context?
5. **Application:** Apply an idea to a short practical example or case study.

For smaller batches, prioritize the first four types and use a scenario only if it tests an important point not already asked. For a larger batch, repeat the mix. Do not turn direct questions into case studies just to make them sound practical. Avoid successive questions with the same opening or structure.

Treat this as a coverage goal, not a reason to invent unsupported material. When a document has no valid comparison or advantage, use a different direct question from the document instead.

## Write clear exam questions

- Prefer short, direct stems such as "What is ...?", "Why is ... useful?", "Which is an advantage of ...?", and "How does ... differ from ...?" where appropriate.
- Keep each question at the selected difficulty. Difficulty changes the depth of reasoning, not the proportion of scenario questions; a challenging question can directly compare concepts or ask about a trade-off.
- Make the four options plausible, distinct, and unambiguous, with one correct answer. Explain the correct idea and the misconception behind each wrong option.
- Use plain language while retaining the proper academic term. Test understanding rather than obscure trivia or wording tricks.
